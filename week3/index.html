Why an MLP struggles with images

Far too many weights

A modest 200 × 200 colour image is 120,000 inputs. One Dense layer of 1,000 units needs 120 million weights: slow, memory-hungry, and easy to overfit.

No idea of neighbourhood

Week 2's thought experiment: shuffle all pixels with one fixed permutation and the MLP trains just as well. It never used the fact that neighbouring pixels belong together.

Position matters too much

Move a pattern a few pixels and it lands on different inputs, so different weights. An MLP must learn every feature separately at every position.

The same shape, moved a few pixels. To a Dense layer these two inputs have almost nothing in common.

  • Three problems, one cause: the MLP throws away the grid structure.
  • Point at the figure: same shape, nearly disjoint sets of active inputs. A CNN will use the same weights at both positions.

Two ideas behind convolution

Local connections

Each output looks only at a small patch of the input, typically 3 × 3. Nearby pixels are combined first; distant ones only in deeper layers.

Shared weights

The same small set of weights, the kernel, is used at every position. A vertical edge at the top left is detected by exactly the weights that detect one at the bottom right.

Together they give translation equivariance: move the pattern, and its response in the output moves with it. The idea goes back to receptive fields in the visual cortex (Hubel and Wiesel, 1962) and to LeNet (LeCun et al., 1998).

200 × 200 × 3 inputParameters
Dense layer, 1,000 units120,001,000
Conv layer, 32 kernels of 3 × 3896

Each kernel spans all 3 colour channels: 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 weights plus 1 bias. 32 kernels: 32 × 28 = 896.

The conv layer does not connect every input to every output, and it reuses its weights. That is a strong assumption about images, and it is the right one.

  • Local plus shared is the whole trick. The parameter count drops by five orders of magnitude.
  • Equivariance, not invariance: the map moves with the input. Pooling and the dense head add (some) invariance later.
  • The Week 3 PDF says 27 parameters per kernel: that leaves out the bias, which makes 28.

Stride and padding

Stride is how far the kernel moves each step. Stride 2 halves the output size.

Padding adds a border of zeros. "valid": none, so the output shrinks by \(k-1\). "same": enough to keep the size at stride 1.

\[ n_{\text{out}} = \left\lfloor \frac{n + 2p - k}{s} \right\rfloor + 1 \]

28 × 28, 3 × 3, valid, stride 126 × 26
28 × 28, 3 × 3, same, stride 128 × 28
200 × 200, 3 × 3, same, stride 2100 × 100
  • Drag k and s; switch padding. The row shows one line of the image; brackets are the kernel's positions.
  • "same" padding p = (k − 1)/2 for odd k. Keras default is "valid".

Many kernels, many channels

A kernel always spans all input channels: on an RGB image a 3 × 3 kernel is really 3 × 3 × 3. Each kernel produces one feature map; a layer with \(F\) kernels outputs \(F\) channels.

\[ \text{parameters} = (k \cdot k \cdot C_{\text{in}} + 1) \cdot F \]

Conv2D(32, 3) on RGB(27 + 1) × 32 = 896
Conv2D(64, 3) on 32 channels(288 + 1) × 64 = 18,496
 
layers.Conv2D(32, kernel_size=3, strides=1,

The number of parameters does not depend on the image size: the same layer works on 28 × 28 or 1,000 × 1,000 images.

  • Common confusion: a "3 × 3 kernel" on 64 input channels has 576 weights. Depth always matches the input.
  • Ask: how many parameters for Conv2D(128, 3) on 64 channels? (576 + 1) × 128 = 73,856.

Pooling: keep the strongest response

Max pooling slides a 2 × 2 window with stride 2 and keeps the largest value. It halves height and width, keeps one value in four, and has no parameters.

  • less computation in later layers
  • each later kernel sees a larger part of the image
  • small shifts of a feature often do not change the output

Move the detected feature and watch the pooled map:

  • Left: the Lecture 5 example: 8, 7, 5, 9.
  • Right: move the bright cell inside its 2 × 2 window: the pooled map does not change. Cross a window border: it moves. Pooling gives robustness to small shifts, not full translation invariance.

Deeper layers see more of the image

The receptive field of a unit is the part of the input it depends on. Each 3 × 3 convolution adds 2 pixels per stride step so far; each pooling doubles the step.

This is how a CNN goes from edges to shapes to objects: early units see a few pixels, deep units see most of the image.

  • Rule: r grows by (k − 1) × j, where j is the product of the strides so far.
  • Link to Lecture 5's hierarchy: edges, then corners and shapes, then parts, then objects.

The CNN architecture

Feature extraction

Repeat Conv, ReLU, Pool. Height and width shrink, the number of channels grows: fewer, richer features.

Flatten

Turn the final 3-D tensor into a vector. GlobalAveragePooling2D is a common alternative: one average per channel, far fewer weights.

Classification head

Dense layers exactly as in Week 2, ending in the output layer and loss chosen from the question.

  • Read the shapes left to right: 28 → 26 → 13 → 11 → 5 → 3 in size, 1 → 32 → 64 → 128 in depth.
  • Lecture 5's 200 × 200 example follows the same pattern with "same" padding: 100 × 100 × 32, 50 × 50 × 64, 25 × 25 × 128, then 80,000 values after flattening.

Ask the room

1. Shuffle every image's pixels with the same fixed permutation, then train both models again. What happens to each?

The MLP is unaffected: it never used pixel order. The CNN collapses towards MLP accuracy or worse, because its kernels rely on neighbouring pixels belonging together. The CNN's advantage is its assumption about images.

2. A CNN recognises a digit wherever it appears. Will it also recognise the digit rotated by 90°, or at twice the size?

Not reliably. Shared weights handle shifts; nothing in the architecture handles rotation or scale. Those have to be learned from examples, which is what data augmentation (Week 2) provides.

  • Give pairs a minute per question before revealing.
  • Both answers make the same point: architecture encodes assumptions; data must cover everything else.

Opening the black box

Deep networks are often called black boxes. In engineering that is not good enough:

Your classifier flags a component as defective. Was it the crack, or a shadow next to it?

Fortunately, CNNs are among the easiest networks to inspect: their internal representations are images too, laid out on the same grid as the input.

Intermediate activations

What does each layer detect? Show the feature maps a layer produces for one image, channel by channel.

Class activation maps (Grad-CAM)

Where did the network look? A heatmap of the regions that drove one specific decision.

Both are cheap to compute for any trained Keras CNN.

  • Frame: inspection tools for models, the same way we inspect structures. Medical imaging, quality control and autonomous systems all need explanations.

Feature maps: one image, many filtered versions

A toy two-layer network with hand-made kernels, so we know what each map should show. A trained network learns its own kernels; its maps look similar in early layers and far more abstract in deep ones.

  • Layer 1 maps are still recognisably the plate: edges in each direction, a blurred copy, thin dark lines.
  • Layer 2 maps are smaller (pooled) and more selective: the "thin line" map lights up only for the crack. Switch to the shadow and the clean plate: the crack map goes dark; edges of the hole stay.
  • That contrast, same filters on different images, is exactly how activations are used for debugging.

The hierarchy of features

Early layers

Edges, lines, colours, simple gradients.

The input is still clearly recognisable.

Middle layers

Textures, simple shapes, parts of objects: corners, circles, a bolt head, a wheel.

Increasingly abstract.

Deep layers

Class-specific concepts: "crack", "weld bead", "cat face".

Sparse: many channels are blank for a given image.

A CNN is an information distillation pipeline: it discards what does not matter for the task (lighting, background) and amplifies what does.

Train the same architecture twice and the individual filters differ, but the overall behaviour is similar. Do not over-interpret one channel.

  • The four practical observations from the reading: first layers keep most of the information; middle layers look like texture and shape detectors; deep layers are sparse; individual filters vary between runs.

Debugging uses: check the network detects edges and shapes as expected, spot dead filters that never activate, and catch a model attending to the background instead of the object.

  • Normalise each channel before plotting if the colours look flat.

Grad-CAM: where did the network look?

Activations show what is detected. Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM; Selvaraju et al., 2017) shows where the network looked to make one specific decision.

  1. Forward pass: get the score \(y^c\) for class \(c\), and the last conv layer's feature maps \(A^k\).
  2. Backward pass: how much would \(y^c\) change if each value of each map changed? \(\partial y^c / \partial A^k\).
  3. Average each map's gradients: one importance weight per channel, \(\alpha_k\).
  4. Weighted sum of the maps, keep the positive part, resize to the image and overlay.

\[ \alpha_k = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A^k_{ij}}, \qquad L^c = \mathrm{ReLU}\Big(\sum_k \alpha_k A^k\Big) \]

Why the last conv layer?

It is the deepest layer that still has a spatial layout. After it, Flatten or pooling throws the "where" away.

Resolution

The heatmap is coarse, often 7 × 7 or 14 × 14, because it comes from a deep layer. It is upsampled for display: it shows regions, not precise outlines.

Analogy: cover parts of the image with your hand and watch the score. Grad-CAM estimates that for every region at once, with one backward pass.

  • Intuition first (the hand analogy), then the four steps, then the formula.
  • The next slide is the hand analogy made literal: occlusion sensitivity.

Try it: cover part of the image

Drag the grey square over the image.

The map shows how much the model's score drops when each region is covered: occlusion sensitivity (Zeiler and Fergus, 2014). Grad-CAM approximates this from gradients, in one pass.

  • Model A: covering the crack drops P(defective) sharply; covering the shadow does nothing. Its map lights up the crack.
  • Model B was "trained" on data where defective parts were also photographed in poor light: its full map lights up dark regions (the shadow, and even the bolt hole and slot), and covering the shadow drops its score. On "Shadow only" it calls a sound plate defective. Same accuracy on a biased test set; very different reasons. Both are toy models with hand-made features.

Grad-CAM in Keras

import tensorflow as tf    # this snippet needs the TF backend

last_conv = model.get_layer("conv2d_2")      # the last Conv2D
grad_model = keras.Model(model.inputs,
                         [last_conv.output, model.output])

with tf.GradientTape() as tape:
    maps, preds = grad_model(image[None])    # model(x), not predict
    c = tf.argmax(preds[0])                  # or a class you choose
    score = preds[:, c]

grads = tape.gradient(score, maps)           # d score / d A
alpha = tf.reduce_mean(grads, axis=(0, 1, 2))   # one per channel
cam = tf.nn.relu(tf.reduce_sum(maps[0] * alpha, axis=-1))
cam = (cam / (tf.reduce_max(cam) + 1e-8)).numpy()   # 0 to 1

plt.imshow(image.squeeze(), cmap="gray")
plt.imshow(cam, cmap="jet", alpha=0.4,
           extent=(0, image.shape[1], image.shape[0], 0))

Call the model, not predict

model.predict(x) is for inference and does not track gradients. Inside a GradientTape, use model(x).

Preprocess exactly as in training

Same scaling, same size, same channel order. OpenCV loads images as BGR, not RGB. If heatmaps look random, check preprocessing first.

Find the layer name with model.summary().

  • This is the keras.io Grad-CAM recipe, trimmed. With pretrained models from keras.applications, use that model's own preprocess_input.
  • With the JAX or PyTorch backend, use that backend's gradient function instead of GradientTape.
  • The extent argument stretches the coarse map over the full image.

Case study: wolf or husky?

Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin (2016) trained a classifier to tell wolves from huskies, deliberately choosing training photos where every wolf stood in snow.

It scored well on similar test photos. Their explanation method (LIME, a relative of the maps on the previous slides) showed why: it was looking at the snow, not the animal. A husky in snow became a "wolf".

The lesson: high test accuracy does not mean the model learned the right thing, if the test set shares the training set's bias. Only an explanation reveals it.

The same trap in engineering

  • all defect photos taken on one rig, under one lamp: the model learns the lamp
  • a scale bar, timestamp or inspector's mark appears mostly on defective samples
  • X-rays of sick patients come from one hospital's scanner, with its own text in the corner

This is Week 2's "chance correlation" again, now visible as a heatmap.

  • Attribution: the wolf/husky experiment is from the LIME paper, and the bias was introduced on purpose to test whether people could spot it from explanations. The PDF credits Grad-CAM; the idea transfers, but the original used LIME.

More detail needs more labelling

The output's detail sets the cost of the training data. A class label takes a second; a box per object takes several; a pixel mask per object can take minutes per image.

Choose the simplest task that answers the engineering question. If "is there a crack?" is enough, do not collect masks.

Other tasks you will meet

Keypoint and pose estimation, depth estimation, tracking objects across video frames, and anomaly detection when defects are too rare to label.

  • Labelling cost is often the real constraint in engineering projects: specialists' time is expensive.

Detection: classify and locate every object

For each object in the image, a detector outputs:

  • a class: what it is
  • a bounding box: where it is, as four numbers
  • a confidence: how sure the model is

The hard part is that the number of objects is not known in advance. A network has a fixed number of outputs, so detectors predict a large, fixed set of candidate boxes and then filter them.

Why it is harder than classification

  • zero, one or hundreds of objects per image
  • objects at very different sizes, from a distant cone to a nearby excavator
  • objects overlapping and partly hidden
  • the box must be accurate, not just the label

Two tools make it work: IoU to compare boxes, and NMS to remove duplicates.

  • Detection as "an advanced form of classification": a classifier per candidate region, plus a regressor for the box.

Bounding boxes and intersection over union

A box is four numbers. Common formats: corners xyxy \((x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2)\), or corner plus size xywh, or center_xywh. Always check which one a library expects.

\[ \mathrm{IoU} = \frac{\text{area of overlap}}{\text{area of union}} \]

Drag the solid box. A detection usually counts as correct at IoU ≥ 0.5.

  • Drag until IoU just reaches 0.5: notice how much the boxes can disagree and still count. That is why COCO also averages over stricter thresholds up to 0.95.

Non-maximum suppression: one box per object

A detector proposes many overlapping boxes for the same object. NMS keeps the best and removes the rest:

  1. drop boxes below the confidence threshold
  2. keep the highest-scoring box left
  3. remove every box that overlaps it with IoU above the threshold
  4. repeat until no boxes are left

  • Step through once, then try an IoU threshold of 0.1 (too aggressive: the two overlapping workers merge into one) and 0.9 (too lenient: duplicates survive).
  • In KerasCV these are the iou_threshold and confidence_threshold of the NonMaxSuppression layer.

Two families of detectors

Two-stage: propose, then classify

First find regions that might contain an object, then classify and refine each one. R-CNN (Girshick et al., 2014), Fast R-CNN (2015), Faster R-CNN (Ren et al., 2015).

Strength: accurate, especially for small objects. Weakness: slower, more complex.

One-stage: predict everything at once

One network predicts classes and boxes for a dense grid of locations in a single forward pass. YOLO (Redmon et al., 2016), SSD (Liu et al., 2016), RetinaNet (Lin et al., 2017).

Strength: fast enough for real-time video. Modern versions are also very accurate.

A third family uses transformers to predict a set of boxes directly, without NMS: DETR (Carion et al., 2020) and its real-time successors.

  • Historical trade-off: two-stage accurate, one-stage fast. Since around 2020 the best one-stage detectors match two-stage accuracy at real-time speed, which is why YOLO dominates in practice.

YOLO: you only look once

YOLO (Redmon et al., 2016) splits the image into an \(S \times S\) grid. The cell that contains an object's centre is responsible for it. Each cell predicts:

  • \(B\) boxes, each \((x, y, w, h)\) plus a confidence
  • \(C\) class probabilities

All cells in one forward pass: output \(S \times S \times (5B + C)\). The original: \(7 \times 7 \times 30\), with \(B = 2\) and the 20 Pascal VOC classes.

  • Click cells: a cell containing an object centre shows the box it predicts; other cells predict "nothing" with low confidence.
  • Then NMS removes duplicates from neighbouring cells. One pass, hence the speed: the 2016 paper ran at 45 frames per second.

Inside a modern YOLO

Backbone

A CNN, exactly what Section 1 built, producing feature maps at strides 8, 16 and 32: fine maps for small objects, coarse maps for large ones.

Neck

A feature pyramid that mixes the scales, so fine maps also get deep semantics. In KerasCV, fpn_depth sets its depth.

Head

At every location of every scale: class scores and a box. For a 640 × 640 input that is 80² + 40² + 20² = 8,400 candidates, then NMS.

  • YOLOv8 specifics: anchor-free (predicts box distances from each location directly), decoupled heads for class and box, trained with a classification loss plus a box loss (CIoU).
  • The grid of slide "YOLO: you only look once" survives as the per-location predictions, now at three scales.

The YOLO family

Versions come from different groups: the original authors (v1 to v3), academic groups (v4, v7, v9, v10, YOLOX), and the company Ultralytics (v5, v8, YOLO11). The ideas accumulate: anchors, multi-scale heads, then anchor-free heads and NMS-free training.

Development continues with new releases; check the Ultralytics documentation for the current version. This module uses YOLOv8, which has a maintained Keras implementation.

  • Not examinable: the point is that "YOLO" is a family and a design idea, not one model.
  • Ultralytics models do detection, segmentation, pose and classification with one API, but they are PyTorch-based.

Measuring a detector: precision, recall and mAP

A detection is a true positive if its class is right and IoU ≥ 0.5 with an unmatched ground-truth box; otherwise it is a false positive. Missed objects are false negatives.

\[ \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \]

AP is the area under the precision-recall curve for one class; mAP averages it over classes. Pascal VOC uses IoU 0.5; COCO averages over IoU thresholds 0.5 to 0.95.

  • Toy data: 15 detections of one class, 10 objects in the images. Lower the threshold: recall rises, precision falls.
  • Always ask which mAP a paper reports: mAP@0.5 and COCO mAP@0.5:0.95 are very different numbers for the same model.

About 26 million parameters; the model card reports an mAP of 0.45 on the Pascal VOC evaluation set.

pad_to_aspect_ratio=True resizes without stretching the image, so boxes keep their shape.

The 20 classes include person, car, bus, bicycle and train, but nothing engineering-specific: no helmet, no crack. For those you train your own.

VOC_CLASSES: the 20 class names in alphabetical order, "aeroplane" to "tvmonitor". KerasCV needs the TensorFlow backend, which is Keras's default.

Transfer learning: start from a backbone pretrained on COCO, so a few hundred labelled images can be enough.

Augmentation must move the boxes too: KerasCV's RandomFlip and JitteredResize take a bounding_box_format and transform boxes with the image.

Week 2 still applies: a validation set split by site or camera, early stopping, and one final test.

Boxes are labelled with tools such as CVAT or Label Studio, and exported in a standard format.

  • The dictionary-of-labels format and the compile arguments follow KerasCV's own example; checked to run on Keras 3.
  • gradient clipping (global_clipnorm) matters: box losses can spike early in training.

Detection in engineering, and what goes wrong

Where it is used

  • site safety: people, helmets and vests on camera feeds
  • inspection: cracks, spalling and corrosion from drone images of bridges and towers
  • manufacturing: missing or misplaced parts on a production line
  • traffic: counting and classifying vehicles at a junction

What goes wrong

  • small objects at the limit of the image resolution
  • rare classes: few examples of the defect that matters most
  • domain shift: new site, lighting, weather or camera
  • inconsistent labels: where does a crack's box end?
  • shortcuts, found with the Section 2 tools

Report mAP and look at the detections, per class and per condition, before trusting a detector on site.

  • Ask students which of these would hit their own project hardest.

Key takeaways

  1. Convolution slides one small kernel over the image: local connections and shared weights, so far fewer parameters and built-in respect for the grid.
  2. Pooling and depth grow the receptive field: edges, then shapes, then objects.
  3. Feature maps and Grad-CAM show what a CNN detects and where it looked. Use them to catch shortcuts before deployment.
  4. Vision tasks differ in their output and labels: one label, boxes, or pixel masks.
  5. YOLO predicts all boxes in one pass, then NMS; judge it with IoU-based precision, recall and mAP.
