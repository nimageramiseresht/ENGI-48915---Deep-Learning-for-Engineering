Humans think in granules, not numbers

Nobody shouts the mass and the speed. We shout heavy and falling: imprecise, but fast, meaningful and enough to act on. Each word is an information granule; how well 126 kg fits heavy is a matter of degree, which is exactly what fuzzy logic models.

  • Tell the story: a colleague does not say "a 126 kg mass is falling at 15 m/s; you have 0.8 s to move at least 5 m". They say "watch out, something heavy is falling on you!"
  • Drag the mass and speed: four fuzzy rules (heavy or light, fast or slow) fire to different degrees, with AND as the minimum, and the strongest rule chooses the warning. A small, fast object and a heavy, slow one now get different warnings.

Partial membership: cold or warm?

Crisp world: one threshold at 25 °C. Below it is cold, above it is warm. So 24.5 °C is exactly as cold as 5 °C, and completely different from 25.5 °C.

Fuzzy world: a temperature belongs to cold and to warm at the same time, each to a degree. 24.5 °C is barely more cold than warm, much like 25.5 °C, and nothing like 5 °C.

Here the fuzzy degrees always add to 1, but they need not: membership is a degree of compatibility, not a probability.

Information granules and granular computing

Information granule

A collection of elements drawn together by their closeness: resemblance, proximity or functionality, in space, in time or in function (Zadeh, 1997). Comfortable temperature, rush hour, small crack.

Granular computing

Representing, constructing, processing and communicating information granules (Pedrycz, 2021, ch. 1). It brings intervals, fuzzy sets, rough sets and others under one banner.

Four observations

  1. Granules are the building blocks of how we represent and process knowledge.
  2. Their size, the level of granularity, shapes how a problem is described and solved.
  3. Granules form hierarchies: the same system seen at coarser or finer detail.
  4. There is no universal level of granularity: it depends on the problem and the user.

Too much detail hides the big picture: we recognise a face, not 2 million pixel values.

  • The four points are Pedrycz's (a) to (d) in section 1.1.
  • Ask: at what granularity does a site manager think about a project schedule? Days? Weeks? Phases? It depends on the decision.

One concept, several kinds of granule

  • Same concept, comfortable room temperature, five formal frameworks from section 1.2.
  • Shadowed sets: three regions, in, out and unknown. Rough sets: a lower and an upper approximation forced by a coarse vocabulary (here, readings only to the nearest 2 °C). The histogram is Pedrycz's Example 1: a granule built from data.

What kind of uncertainty?

After Klir and Yuan (1995).

TypeExampleNatural granule
Fuzziness (vagueness)Is 126 kg heavy? Is 23 °C warm?fuzzy set
Nonspecificity (imprecision)the load is somewhere between 100 and 150 kginterval, set

Fuzziness is not probability

Probability asks how likely an event is: will it rain on 1 September? Fuzziness asks how well a value fits a concept: is 10 °C warm? Nothing random happens; the boundary of the word is gradual. The two combine: "high probability of low rainfall".

  • The taxonomy is Klir and Yuan's figure, also in the CIVE 654 notes.
  • Ask the room for an engineering example of each: vague specifications ("adequate drainage"), imprecise measurements (± tolerance), conflicting expert opinions.

Why granules matter for AI: explanation

Modern AI models are accurate but opaque. To be trusted, their results must be explained at a level of abstraction people use: as information granules.

"The pump is likely to fail soon because vibration is high and temperature is rising quickly" is an explanation. A 64-dimensional activation vector is not.

Granules sit at the interface between a model and its users: inputs described in their terms, outputs delivered in their terms.

  • Pedrycz, section 1.3, explainable AI, and section 1.5: a processing framework with granular interfaces.
  • This is the thread we come back to in Section 7: granules as a way of putting domain knowledge into, and getting explanation out of, a neural network.

Fuzzy sets: a departure from yes or no

A classical set draws a sharp line: 21.9 °C is comfortable, 22.1 °C is not. A fuzzy set admits degrees of membership (Zadeh, 1965):

\[ A : X \to [0, 1] \]

\(A(x) = 1\): fully compatible with the concept; \(A(x) = 0\): incompatible; values in between: partly compatible. The membership function is the fuzzy set.

Neighbouring concepts overlap: 25 °C is partly comfortable and partly warm.

Precision in the grades is not the point: \(A(4) = 0.9865\) means nothing more than "high". What matters is the ordering.

  • Pedrycz's own example (section 2.2): comfortable around 20 °C; 19.5 and 20.5 feel the same to people; 25 °C is partly comfortable and partly warm.
  • The crisp interval sits under the axis for comparison: drag across 22 °C and the crisp answer flips while the fuzzy grades change gradually.

Fuzzy sets on a discrete universe

The universe need not be continuous. Here \(X\) is the ten provinces of Canada, and the fuzzy set extreme winter cold gives each one a degree:

\[ A = \tfrac{0}{\text{BC}} + \tfrac{0.47}{\text{AB}} + \tfrac{0.71}{\text{SK}} + \tfrac{0.80}{\text{MB}} + \dots + \tfrac{0.14}{\text{NL}} \]

Read \(\tfrac{a}{x}\) as "\(x\) with membership \(a\)"; the + only lists the elements. On a finite universe a fuzzy set is simply a vector of grades, one per element.

The crisp version must pick a cut-off: with −10 °C, Alberta (−10.4) counts as extremely cold and New Brunswick (−9.4) does not.

Grades from the January mean temperature of each capital (approximate), through \(A(T) = 1\) below −20 °C, 0 above −2 °C, linear in between.

  • Temperatures are approximate January means for the provincial capitals; check them against Environment Canada climate normals before teaching. The territories would all be close to 1.
  • Switch to crisp: one degree decides whether Alberta is "extremely cold". Ask the room whether Winnipeg and Edmonton are equally cold in January.

Common membership functions

\[ A(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x - a}{m - a}, & x \in [a, m] \\[4pt] \dfrac{b - x}{b - m}, & x \in [m, b] \\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

The simplest model: a modal value \(m\) and bounds \(a\), \(b\). Three parameters, clear meaning.

\[ A(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{x - a}{m - a}, & x \in [a, m] \\[4pt] 1, & x \in [m, n] \\[4pt] \dfrac{b - x}{b - n}, & x \in [n, b] \\[4pt] 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

A flat top \([m, n]\): those values are equally typical, as in a tolerance band.

\[ A(x) = \exp\!\big(-(x - m)^2 / \sigma^2\big) \]

Smooth and differentiable everywhere, which suits gradient-based learning; \(\sigma\) sets the spread.

\[ A(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x \le a \\ 2\big(\tfrac{x - a}{b - a}\big)^2, & x \in [a, m] \\ 1 - 2\big(\tfrac{x - b}{b - a}\big)^2, & x \in [m, b] \\ 1, & x \ge b \end{cases} \]

Monotonic, for open-ended concepts such as high or large; \(m = (a + b)/2\) is the crossover point.

Straight lines between points \((a_i, b_i)\) given by an expert: the most flexible shape, and the natural result of eliciting a few membership grades.

Choose the shape to match the meaning of the concept, the detail you need, and whether you will optimise it later.

  • Pedrycz section 2.3. Drag the parameters for each shape.
  • Sigmoid functions are the other common monotonic choice; they satisfy dA/dx ∝ A(1 − A) (section 2.3, Example 2).

Fuzzy numbers

A fuzzy number is a fuzzy set on the real line, \(A : \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]\), with two properties (Dubois and Prade, 1978):

  • Normal: its height is 1, so at least one value is fully typical: \[ \mathrm{hgt}(A) = \sup_{x} A(x) = 1 \]
  • Unimodal: one peak; membership rises to the top, then falls, and never dips in between.

Fuzzy numbers model imprecise quantities: about 20 minutes, around 120 kg, roughly 3 m. Triangular, trapezoidal and Gaussian shapes are the usual choices.

  • Click through the four candidates. Only the first two are fuzzy numbers: the third never reaches 1, the fourth has two peaks.
  • A flat-topped trapezoid is sometimes called a fuzzy interval; it is still normal and unimodal.

Height, normality, support and core

Height, \(\mathrm{hgt}(A) = \sup_x A(x)\). If it is 1 the set is normal: at least one element is fully typical. Otherwise it is subnormal, and can be normalised by dividing by its height.

Support, \(\mathrm{supp}(A) = \{x : A(x) > 0\}\): everything with any membership at all.

Core, \(\mathrm{core}(A) = \{x : A(x) = 1\}\): the fully typical elements.

Convex if \(A(\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda)x_2) \ge \min(A(x_1), A(x_2))\): no dips between two points.

  • Lower the height: the core disappears (empty) because nothing reaches 1, and the set becomes subnormal.
  • Typo to watch for in the book's eq. 2.12: the core is the set where A(x) = 1, not 0.

α-cuts and the representation theorem

An α-cut keeps the elements whose membership reaches α. It is an ordinary set, an interval for a fuzzy number:

\[ A_\alpha = \{x : A(x) \ge \alpha\} \]

Raising α gives smaller, nested cuts: \(\alpha \le \beta \Rightarrow A_\alpha \supseteq A_\beta\). The support is the limit at 0, the core the cut at 1.

Representation theorem: a fuzzy set is exactly the stack of its α-cuts, each lifted to height α:

\[ A(x) = \sup_{\alpha \in (0, 1]} \alpha\, A_\alpha(x) \]

So anything we can compute for intervals, we can compute for fuzzy sets, one α at a time.

  • This is why fuzzy-number arithmetic works with interval arithmetic, and why the principle of justifiable granularity (Section 6) can build a fuzzy set out of intervals.
  • The book's sentence that "higher values of the threshold imply that more elements are accepted" has it backwards: higher α accepts fewer.

Cardinality, energy and entropy

Cardinality (σ-count): add up the grades, counting partial members partly.

\[ \mathrm{card}(A) = \sum_{x} A(x) \]

Energy: weight each grade by an increasing \(e\), with \(e(0) = 0\), \(e(1) = 1\). With \(e(u) = u^2\), grades near 1 count much more than grades near 0.5.

\[ E(A) = \sum_x e\big(A(x)\big) \]

Entropy: how fuzzy the set is. \(h\) is 0 at 0 and 1, and largest at ½, the most hesitant grade; here \(h(u) = 2\min(u, 1 - u)\).

\[ H(A) = \sum_x h\big(A(x)\big) \]

  • The two sets are chosen to have the same cardinality, 3.0. Click Energy: B wins, because its grades are all 0 or 1. Click Entropy: A wins by far, because its grades all hover around 0.5; B, a crisp set, has zero entropy.
  • The outlined bars show each element's contribution e(u) or h(u); the shaded bars are the grades themselves.

Specificity: how much detail does a granule carry?

Specificity is a decreasing function of the size of a granule, equal to 1 for a single number and 0 for the whole range. For an interval:

\[ \mathrm{sp}(A) = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{length}(A)}{\mathrm{range}} \]

For a fuzzy set, average the specificity of its α-cuts (representation theorem again):

\[ \mathrm{sp}(A) = \int_0^1 \mathrm{sp}(A_\alpha)\, d\alpha \]

A symmetric triangle of half-width \(s\) has cuts of length \(2s(1 - \alpha)\), so \(\mathrm{sp}(A) = 1 - s/\mathrm{range}\).

Specificity and cardinality pull opposite ways: the more a granule includes, the more general and the less specific it is.

  • Range here is [0, 20]. Drag s to 0: specificity 1, a single number. Drag to 10: the triangle spans the range and specificity falls to 0.5, since the α-cuts shrink towards the top.
  • Other decreasing functions work too, such as exp(−length); the paper in Section 7 uses an exponential form.

Coverage, and families of fuzzy sets

A variable is usually described by a family of fuzzy sets, such as low, medium, high: a frame of cognition. To keep its meaning (Pedrycz, 2021, §2.6):

  • Coverage: every \(x\) belongs to some set, \(\max_i A_i(x) \ge \varepsilon\)
  • Few sets: at most about 7 ± 2, so each keeps a name
  • Unimodal sets, each with one focus
  • Restricted overlap: \(\max_x \min(A_i(x), A_j(x)) \le \delta\), so neighbours stay distinguishable
  • Narrow sets leave gaps (coverage fails); wide sets overlap so much that neighbours mean the same thing; 12 sets are too many to name.
  • Thresholds used here: ε = 0.2 and δ = 0.75. The book prints the overlap condition with "> δ"; it should be "at most δ".

Where do membership functions come from?

From people

Elicit the meaning of a concept from experts: voting, intervals, or pairwise comparisons (Pedrycz, 2021, ch. 8).

Captures domain knowledge; subjective, and hard with many variables.

From data

Let the structure in the data define the granules: clustering, an unsupervised method (ch. 9).

Objective and automatic; ignores what experts know, and granules may lack meaning.

From both

Summarise data with granules that are both supported by evidence and specific enough to mean something: justifiable granularity (ch. 10).

The middle road: data plus a design criterion.

Sections 5 and 6 build granules from data. First, two expert-driven methods, and then the idea of learning without labels.

  • Frame this as a spectrum: human-centric at one end, data-driven at the other, justifiable granularity in between (the book's own framing in the chapter 10 abstract).

From experts: horizontal and vertical methods

Horizontal

For each value \(x\), ask \(N\) experts "is \(x\) heavy?" and take the share of yes answers as the membership: \(A(x) = N_{\text{yes}} / N\). Disagreement gives intermediate grades.

Vertical

For chosen levels \(\alpha\), ask "which values are heavy to degree at least \(\alpha\)?". Each answer is an α-cut; the representation theorem stacks them into a fuzzy set.

Saaty's pairwise comparisons ask only "how much heavier is A than B?", and check the answers for consistency (§8.5).

  • The demo simulates 10 experts with slightly different personal thresholds for "heavy". Re-poll: the curve wobbles; with more experts it would smooth out.
  • These are the methods in CIVE 654 Lecture 9 as direct and indirect methods; the book calls them horizontal and vertical (section 8.4).

Unsupervised learning: structure without labels

Supervised learning, everything so far in this module, learns a mapping from input-output pairs: a classifier or a regressor.

Unsupervised learning has inputs only. Its job is to reveal structure: groups, regularities, a summary of the data. Clustering is the classic example: find \(c\) groups of similar data, each described by a prototype \(\mathbf{v}_i\).

In granular computing, every cluster is an information granule built from data, with a meaning the data supplies.

  • Engineering examples: grouping operating conditions of a machine, finding typical daily load profiles, segmenting traffic patterns, none of which come with labels.

From hard to fuzzy partitions

Drag the points. Prototypes are held fixed for illustration.

A clustering of \(N\) points into \(c\) groups is a partition matrix \(U\), \(c \times N\), with \(u_{ik}\) the membership of point \(k\) in cluster \(i\):

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{c} u_{ik} = 1, \qquad 0 < \sum_{k=1}^{N} u_{ik} < N \]

  • Hard view: each point goes to its nearest prototype, so its column holds a single 1. The starting layout gives the book's own example (section 9.2): cluster 1 holds x2, x4 and x5.
  • Switch to fuzzy: x3, sitting between clusters 1 and 2, gets about half of each. Drag it towards a prototype and watch its column sharpen; drag it to the middle of the triangle and it spreads across all three.
  • Memberships use the FCM formula with m = 2.

The FCM objective

Given data \(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and a number of clusters \(c\), find prototypes \(\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_c\) and a fuzzy partition matrix \(U\) that minimise

\[ Q = \sum_{i=1}^{c}\sum_{k=1}^{N} u_{ik}^{\,m}\, \|\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{v}_i\|^2 \qquad \text{subject to } \sum_{i=1}^{c} u_{ik} = 1 \]

Each point pulls on every prototype, in proportion to its membership raised to the power \(m\).

The fuzzification coefficient \(m > 1\) controls how soft the partition is; \(m = 2\) is the usual choice.

Solving it: alternate two steps

With \(U\) fixed, setting \(\partial Q / \partial \mathbf{v}_i = 0\) gives weighted means:

\[ \mathbf{v}_i = \frac{\sum_k u_{ik}^{\,m}\, \mathbf{x}_k}{\sum_k u_{ik}^{\,m}} \]

With the prototypes fixed, a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint gives:

\[ u_{ik} = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{c} \left( \dfrac{\|\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{v}_i\|}{\|\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{v}_j\|} \right)^{2/(m - 1)}} \]

  • Derivation in section 9.3: augment Q with λ(Σu − 1) per data point, differentiate, and eliminate λ using the constraint.
  • Read the membership formula: a point twice as far from v_i as from v_j gets a much smaller membership in i; the exponent 2/(m − 1) controls how much smaller.

The FCM algorithm

import numpy as np

def fcm(X, c=3, m=2.0, tol=1e-5, max_iter=300, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    U = rng.random((c, len(X)))
    U /= U.sum(axis=0)                  # each column sums to 1
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        Um = U ** m
        V = Um @ X / Um.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)    # prototypes
        D = np.linalg.norm(X[None] - V[:, None], axis=2) + 1e-12
        U_new = 1 / ((D[:, None] / D[None])
                     ** (2 / (m - 1))).sum(axis=1)  # memberships
        done = np.abs(U_new - U).max() < tol
        U = U_new
        if done:
            break
    Q = ((U ** m) * D ** 2).sum()
    return V, U, Q

V, U, Q = fcm(X, c=3, m=2.0)    # X: (N, n) array of data
  1. Initialise \(U\) at random, columns summing to 1.
  2. Update the prototypes from \(U\).
  3. Update \(U\) from the prototypes.
  4. Stop when \(U\) barely changes, \(\|U^{(t+1)} - U^{(t)}\| < \varepsilon\).

Each iteration never increases \(Q\), so the algorithm converges, though to a local minimum: run it from several seeds.

Tested: on three Gaussian blobs it finds the three centres. scikit-fuzzy offers the same as skfuzzy.cluster.cmeans.

  • Twenty lines that follow equations 9.13 and 9.16 exactly. D[:, None] / D[None] builds every ratio d_ik / d_jk at once.
  • skfuzzy expects data shaped (features, samples), transposed relative to this function.

Try it: fuzzy c-means on two-dimensional data

The fuzzifier m shapes the membership functions

With the prototypes fixed at \(v_1 = -4\), \(v_2 = 0\) and \(v_3 = 2.7\) (Pedrycz, 2021, Fig. 9.6), the membership formula gives a fuzzy set for each cluster, with an explicit formula.

  • \(m \to 1\): characteristic functions, so FCM becomes k-means
  • \(m = 2\): smooth, Gaussian-like sets
  • large \(m\): flat, near \(1/c\), with spikes at the prototypes

Note the rippling: because memberships must sum to 1, a set can rise again far from its prototype. FCM sets are normal but not always unimodal.

  • Far away from all prototypes every membership tends to 1/c, here 1/3. This matters in Section 7: a prediction far outside the data still gets coverage 1/c, which the paper has to correct.

Choosing c, the distance, and reading the result

Number of clusters, c

\(Q\) always falls as \(c\) grows; with \(c = N\), \(Q = 0\). For granules that people can name, keep \(c\) small, about 5 to 9, or use a validity index or subtractive clustering to suggest it.

Distance

It decides the shape of the clusters. Euclidean finds spheres; standardise variables first, or use a weighted distance, \(\sum_j (x_j - v_j)^2 / \sigma_j^2\).

Reading the result

The prototypes are numeric summaries of each granule; the rows of \(U\) are the granules themselves, fuzzy sets over the data. To name the granules variable by variable, project them onto each axis: next slide.

  • Sections 9.4 and 9.5. Subtractive clustering is what the paper in Section 7 used to choose c = 5.
  • Projection is how FCM produces the labelled granules a fuzzy rule-based model needs.

Projecting clusters onto each variable

FCM's granules live in the full space. To talk about one variable at a time, drop each prototype onto that axis (dashed lines) and apply the membership formula on that axis alone:

\[ A_{ij}(x_j) = \frac{1}{\sum_{l=1}^{c} \left( \dfrac{x_j - v_{ij}}{x_j - v_{lj}} \right)^{2/(m-1)}} \]

Each variable gets its own vocabulary, built from data: low, medium, high, ready for rules such as IF x is low AND y is high THEN …

  • Pedrycz section 9.5 and Fig. 9.8. Below the scatter are the fuzzy sets on x; to its left, rotated, those on y.
  • Switch layout: two prototypes now project to almost the same y, so their fuzzy sets on y collapse into one. Only two meaningful granules remain on that variable, although there are three clusters.

Two requirements that pull apart

Forecasting tomorrow's temperature:

StatementCoverageSpecificity
it will be 17.65 °Calmost never truemaximal
between 15 and 18 °Coften truehigh
between 12 and 22 °Cusually truemoderate
between −10 and 34 °Calways trueuseless

A good granule is justified by experimental evidence (coverage) and carries meaning (specificity). The more specific a statement, the less likely it is to hold.

Coverage

How much data stands behind the granule: for an interval, the fraction of data inside it.

\[ \mathrm{cov}(A) = \frac{1}{N}\,\mathrm{card}\{x_k : x_k \in A\} \]

Specificity

How precise the granule is (Section 3):

\[ \mathrm{sp}(A) = 1 - \frac{|b - a|}{\mathrm{range}} \]

  • Pedrycz Fig. 10.1. Ask the room which statement a site manager would want before pouring concrete tomorrow: it is the compromise, not either extreme.

The principle: maximise coverage × specificity

\[ V = \mathrm{cov}(A) \cdot \mathrm{sp}(A), \qquad A^{*} = \arg\max V \]

Two-phase design: take a numeric representative \(r\), such as the mean; then choose each bound separately, using only the data on its side:

\[ b^{*} = \arg\max_b \; \mathrm{cov}([r, b])\,\mathrm{sp}([r, b]) \]

As \(b\) grows, coverage climbs in steps and specificity falls linearly; their product peaks in between.

  • The data are Pedrycz's Example 1 (section 10.1): 16 values with mean 0.756. Move b along the data: the book's optimum is b = 2.5 with V = 0.34; the same procedure on the left gives a = −2.0 (V = 0.38). Verified in Python.
  • Coverage on each side is counted relative to the data on that side, as in the book's worked numbers.

From intervals to fuzzy sets

Weight specificity by a power \(\xi\):

\[ V = \mathrm{cov}(A)\cdot \mathrm{sp}(A)^{\xi} \]

\(\xi = 0\) ignores specificity, so the interval covers everything; larger \(\xi\) gives narrower, more specific intervals. They are nested, like α-cuts.

So rescale \(\xi\) to \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) and stack the intervals: by the representation theorem, the result is a fuzzy set built directly from the data.

Alternatively, fix a shape (such as triangular), replace the count with the σ-count \(\sum_k A(x_k)\), and optimise its bounds.

  • Section 10.2. This closes the loop with Section 3: α-cuts, specificity and cardinality are exactly the tools the principle uses.

Justifiable granularity in Python

import numpy as np

def justifiable_interval(x, r=None):
    """Interval granule [a, b] maximising coverage * specificity."""
    x = np.sort(np.asarray(x, float))
    r = x.mean() if r is None else r          # numeric representative
    up = x[x > r]                             # candidates for b
    lo = x[x < r][::-1]                       # candidates for a
    V_b = (np.arange(1, len(up) + 1) / len(up)          # coverage
           * (1 - (up - r) / (x.max() - r)))           # specificity
    V_a = (np.arange(1, len(lo) + 1) / len(lo)
           * (1 - (r - lo) / (r - x.min())))
    return lo[V_a.argmax()], r, up[V_b.argmax()]

data = [-5.2, -4.5, -2.0, -1.9, -0.5, 0.1, 0.7, 0.8,
        1.7, 1.9, 2.2, 2.5, 3.1, 3.5, 4.4, 5.3]
print(justifiable_interval(data))    # (-2.0, 0.756, 2.5)

Every data value is a candidate bound, so the search is a simple sweep: coverage counts the points passed so far, specificity falls with the distance from \(r\).

Reproduces the book's example: \(b^* = 2.5\) with \(V = 0.342\), and \(a^* = -2.0\).

To summarise an FCM cluster, call it on that cluster's data with \(r = \mathbf{v}_i\), counting each point by its membership.

Three ways granules enter machine learning

Granular inputs

Describe inputs with fuzzy sets and reason with rules: IF vibration is high AND temperature is rising THEN risk is high. Fuzzy rule-based and neuro-fuzzy models (Pedrycz, 2021, chs. 12 to 14).

Gain: interpretable models.

Granular outputs

Predict a granule rather than a number: an interval or fuzzy set around the forecast, built with justifiable granularity.

Gain: honest uncertainty.

Granular knowledge in training

Use granules as knowledge hints in the loss function, so a standard network learns to respect what the granules say is physically valid.

Gain: reliability outside the training data. Our case study.

All three answer the same weakness of purely data-driven models: they memorise statistical patterns, and can produce confident, impossible answers when the data shift.

  • Remind the class of Week 2: a model that overfits learns accidents of the sample; Week 3: shortcuts found by Grad-CAM. Knowledge hints are another defence.

Case study: predicting the drag of space debris

Over a million debris objects of 1 to 10 cm orbit in low Earth orbit. Predicting where they will be depends above all on atmospheric drag, governed by the ballistic coefficient:

\[ \mathrm{BC} = \frac{C_d\, A_{\text{proj}}}{M} \]

A recurrent network (a GRU, Week 4) can estimate BC from 180 days of orbital elements. Purely data-driven, it is accurate on familiar data but can predict a negative BC, implying that drag adds energy to the orbit: physically impossible.

Four kinds of generalisation

  • Type I: interpolation within the training data
  • Type II: the same objects, further in time
  • Type III: unseen objects (new altitude, new shape)
  • Type IV: unseen environments (a solar maximum)

Data: 25,000 synthetic objects with known BC from aerodynamic simulation, filtered to 15,000 observable ones, since real catalogues have no ground-truth BC.

  • Types I to III follow Peng and Bai; Type IV is introduced in the paper.
  • The safety argument: avoiding impossible predictions under domain shift matters more than a few percent of in-distribution accuracy.

Step 1: granulate the target with FCM

Illustrative shapes; the paper's Figure 1 shows the real granules.

Cluster the known physical BC values of the 15,000 objects, the output space, not the inputs. Each cluster is a valid aerodynamic regime.

  • number of granules from subtractive clustering: \(C = 5\)
  • membership functions from FCM with \(m = 2\): Section 5's formula
  • each granule \(G_k\) gets a specificity from its area \(A_k\) relative to the range \(R\):

\[ \mathrm{sp}(G_k) = \exp\!\big(-K_{\text{spec}}\, A_k / R\big) \]

Narrow regimes are specific; broad ones less so.

  • Why the output space: clustering the inputs would only rediscover the Latin hypercube sampling grid used to generate them.
  • Specificity via the area under the membership function is the paper's choice for fuzzy granules, since they have no sharp endpoints (Section 3: specificity decreases with size).

Step 2: a knowledge-informed loss function

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L} = {} & \frac{\lambda}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \|y_k - \hat{y}_k\|^2 \\ & + \frac{1 - \lambda}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \Big(1 - \mathrm{cov}(\hat{y}_k, G_k)\,\mathrm{sp}(G_k)\Big) \end{aligned} \]

The first sum, the data term, is the usual MSE. The second, the knowledge term, rewards a prediction for lying inside the right granule: justifiable granularity's coverage × specificity, now a training signal.

  • Coverage = the FCM membership of the prediction \(\hat{y}_k\) in granule \(G_k\)
  • Target-anchored: \(G_k\) is the granule of the true value \(y_k\), so the network cannot cheat by aiming at the most specific granule
  • Boundary penalty: far from all clusters FCM membership tends to \(1/C\), so below the physical floor coverage is forced down exponentially
  • Boundary: for ŷ below Y_min = 0.05 m²/kg, cov = cov₀(Y_min)·exp(α(ŷ − Y_min)), which gives strong gradients back into the valid region.
  • λ is annealed: start with more knowledge, then shift weight towards the data as the knowledge term stops improving. No extra network, and no differential equations in the loss, unlike a PINN.

Try it: the knowledge term

\[ \mathcal{L}_k = \lambda\,\underbrace{(y_k - \hat{y}_k)^2}_{\text{data}} + (1 - \lambda)\,\underbrace{\big(1 - \mathrm{cov}\cdot\mathrm{sp}\big)}_{\text{knowledge}} \]

\(\lambda\) shares the loss between the two: \(\lambda = 1\) is plain MSE, the baseline; \(\lambda = 0\) is knowledge only. In training it starts low, so the granules first anchor the physics, and rises as the knowledge term stops improving.

  • One sample's loss. Move the prediction away from the truth but inside the same granule: the data term grows, the knowledge term barely moves. Move it into another granule: the knowledge term jumps. Below the floor the penalty takes over.
  • Set λ to 1: pure MSE, the baseline. Set it to 0: knowledge only, which keeps predictions in the right regime but not at the right value. Illustrative granules; K_spec = 1.5, α = 10 as in the paper's in-distribution set-up.

This is the bias-variance trade-off of Week 2: a little bias, in exchange for much less variance where the data run out.

Key takeaways

  1. Information granules represent knowledge at the level of abstraction people use. Fuzzy sets capture vagueness, intervals capture imprecision, probability captures randomness.
  2. Fuzzy sets are membership functions \(A: X \to [0, 1]\), on continuous or discrete universes; fuzzy numbers are the ones on the real line with height 1 and a single peak.
  3. Descriptors summarise a granule: support, core, α-cuts, cardinality, energy, entropy, specificity; families need coverage and distinguishability.
  4. Granules can be built from experts, from data with fuzzy c-means, or with the principle of justifiable granularity, which maximises coverage × specificity.
  5. In machine learning, granules make models interpretable, outputs honest, and training knowledge-informed, which helps most when the data shift.

Looking ahead: granules for structural health monitoring

A CNN with segmentation (Weeks 3 and 4) can find cracks on concrete. Granular computing can help turn those pixels into decisions an inspector trusts. Some directions to explore:

Describe cracks as granules

From each mask, measure width, length, orientation and density. Treat each measurement as a fuzzy number, since a width read from pixels is about 0.3 mm, not exactly.

Categories without hard cut-offs

Build hairline, fine, moderate and severe with FCM or justifiable granularity on measured cracks, anchored to code limits, so a 0.29 mm crack is not a different world from a 0.31 mm one.

Rules an inspector can read

IF width is moderate AND length is long AND location is near a support THEN priority is high. Every classification comes with its reason.

Honest outputs

Report severity as a granule: an interval or fuzzy set reflecting segmentation uncertainty, such as the spread of SAM's candidate masks.

Knowledge in training

As in the debris case, add a knowledge term that penalises physically implausible masks or widths below the camera's resolution.

Granules over time

Track each crack's granules across inspections; an RNN on granular time series can flag cracks that are growing quickly.

  • Deliberately open-ended: these are project ideas, not a method. Each connects a part of today's lecture (fuzzy numbers, FCM, justifiable granularity, rules, the knowledge-informed loss) with the CNN and segmentation weeks.
  • Category boundaries such as crack-width limits come from the relevant design code or inspection manual; the point is to make the transitions gradual.
